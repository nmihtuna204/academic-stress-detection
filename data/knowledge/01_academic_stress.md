# Academic stress in university students in Vietnam

## What is academic stress?

Academic stress is a state of physical and psychological tension arising from the
demands of studying: workload, exams, grades, competition with peers and family
expectations. At moderate levels stress can motivate learning; but when it is
prolonged or exceeds a student's capacity to cope, it negatively affects health,
sleep, memory and academic performance.

## Common sources of stress for students in Vietnam

- **Exam and grade pressure**: packed exam schedules, fear of failing a course,
  outstanding credits, retaking subjects; GPA is tied to scholarships and job
  opportunities.
- **Assignment workload**: back-to-back deadlines, projects, essays and group
  assignments overlapping.
- **Family expectations**: the "other people's children" comparison, pressure to
  succeed so as not to let parents down, especially for students living away from
  home.
- **Financial pressure**: tuition, rent and living costs; many students work
  part-time on top of studying, cutting into their rest.
- **Peer pressure**: comparing yourself with friends on grades, internships,
  language certificates and extracurricular activities.
- **Transition to a new environment**: first-year students have to adapt to
  self-directed learning at university, living away from family, and building new
  relationships.
- **Career direction**: final-year students worry about internships, their thesis,
  job applications and life after graduation.

## Signs of prolonged stress

- **Physical**: insomnia or oversleeping, headaches, persistent fatigue, a racing
  heart, irregular eating.
- **Emotional**: irritability, constant worry, low mood, feeling overwhelmed, loss
  of interest in things you used to enjoy.
- **Cognitive**: difficulty concentrating, forgetfulness, negative thoughts about
  yourself, a sense of being stuck.
- **Behavioural**: procrastination, skipping classes, withdrawing from friends,
  overusing caffeine, uncontrolled social-media scrolling.

## When should you seek professional support?

You should reach out to a mental-health professional or a medical facility when:
symptoms last more than 2 weeks without easing; they clearly affect your study and
daily life; thoughts of harming yourself appear; or you use substances to cope.
Seeking help early is a sign of strength, not weakness.
