# Stress screening scales: DASS-21 and PSS-10

## DASS-21 (Depression Anxiety Stress Scales – 21 items)

The DASS-21 by Lovibond & Lovibond (1995) measures three negative emotional states
over the past week: **depression** (low mood, loss of interest, hopelessness),
**anxiety** (fear, physical reactions such as trembling and a racing heart) and
**stress** (difficulty relaxing, irritability, restlessness). Each subscale has 7
items answered 0–3; each subscale total is doubled and compared against
classification thresholds: Normal / Mild / Moderate / Severe / Extremely Severe.
A validated Vietnamese version of the scale also exists (Tran et al., 2013).

**Important note**: the DASS-21 is a *screening* instrument, not a diagnostic tool.
A high score suggests you should seek a specialist assessment; it does not mean you
have a mental disorder.

## PSS-10 (Perceived Stress Scale – 10 items)

Cohen's PSS-10 measures how far a person *perceives* their life over the past month
as unpredictable, uncontrollable and overloaded. The 10 items are answered on a 0–4
scale (Never → Very often); the positively worded items (4, 5, 7, 8) are reverse
scored. The total runs 0–40: 0–13 low, 14–26 moderate, 27–40 high.

Unlike the DASS-21 (which measures *symptoms*), the PSS-10 measures *subjective
perception* of stress — two complementary perspectives, so it is worth combining
both in a self-assessment.

## Interpreting your results in a healthy way

- Results reflect your **recent** state (the past week for the DASS-21, the past
  month for the PSS-10), not who you are as a person; scores change with
  circumstances.
- High scores during exam season are common; treat them as a signal to adjust the
  balance between study and rest, not as a diagnostic label.
- Persistently high scores, or scores accompanied by thoughts of self-harm, are a
  sign to see a psychologist or doctor soon.
- Do not use self-assessment results to diagnose yourself or self-medicate.
