# Sleep, exams and academic performance

## Why does sleep matter for students?

Sleep is when the brain consolidates memory: what you learn during the day is
written into long-term memory largely while you sleep. Sleep deprivation reduces
concentration, processing speed and emotional regulation — meaning you study less
effectively and get more stressed by the same amount of work.

## All-nighters: more harm than good

Many students stay up all night before an exam. Research shows that after one
sleepless night, memory and reasoning decline by an amount comparable to the
impairment caused by a blood alcohol level above the drink-driving limit. Revising
for 4 hours and then sleeping properly is usually more effective than revising for
8 hours through the night.

## Sleep hygiene tips for students

- Keep consistent bed and wake times, including at weekends (within about an hour).
- Bedroom or dorm room: dark, quiet, well ventilated; use an eye mask or earplugs
  if it is noisy.
- Avoid phone and laptop screens 30 minutes before bed; turn on a blue-light
  filter in the evening.
- No caffeine (coffee, milk tea, energy drinks) after 3pm.
- Do not study in bed — keep the bed for sleep so your brain builds the
  association.
- If you have been lying down for 20 minutes without sleeping: get up, do
  something gentle (read a paper book) until you feel sleepy, then go back to bed.
- A short 15–25 minute nap helps alertness; napping for more than an hour tends to
  cause restlessness at night.

## Signs of a sleep disorder worth noting

Difficulty sleeping or insomnia lasting more than 3 weeks, recurring nightmares, or
excessive daytime sleepiness despite enough hours in bed — these are worth getting
checked, as they can be signs of an anxiety or depressive disorder needing
specialist assessment.
