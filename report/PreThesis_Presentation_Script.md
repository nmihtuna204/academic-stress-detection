## Delivery strategy — read this first

The single most important decision in this talk: **volunteer the weaknesses before
the panel extracts them.** Three findings in this project are genuinely unflattering
— template-derived data, crisis recall of 0.50, and a proposed system with no
reportable score. Each one lands as evidence of rigour if you say it first, and as
a concealment if the panel finds it.

The talk is therefore built in three parts:

| Part | Slides | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — How it is built | 4–8 | Show the engineering is sound |
| 2 — What the measurements say | 9–12 | Show the numbers, including the bad ones |
| 3 — What this does and does not prove | 13–15 | Own the limits before they are raised |

*Ghi chú: đừng giấu ba điểm yếu. Nói trước thì thành "biết tự đánh giá", để hội đồng

## Slide 2 — The problem

**On screen**
- Academic stress is prevalent, under-detected, and under-served
- Existing tools: paper questionnaires (no feedback) *or* chatbots (no instrument)
- Gap: nothing combines a **validated instrument** with **grounded, actionable advice**
- Constraint that shapes everything: getting it wrong can cause harm

**Script (~75 s)**

> Academic stress among university students is common and often goes undetected
> until it becomes a crisis. Two kinds of tools exist today and each is missing
> something.
>
> On one side, paper or web questionnaires like DASS-21. They are validated and
> trusted — but a student fills one in, gets a number, and receives nothing back.
> No explanation, no next step.
>
> On the other side, general-purpose chatbots. They are conversational and give
> advice — but they are not anchored to any validated instrument, and they will
> confidently invent advice that has no source.
>
> The gap this project addresses is the combination: keep the validated
> instrument as the authority, and use a language model only to explain and to
> suggest — grounded in a curated knowledge base rather than in its own
> parameters.
>
> And one constraint shapes every design decision that follows: in this domain,
> a wrong answer is not a bad user experience. It is potential harm. That is why
> the architecture is built the way I will show next.

## Slide 4 — Architecture: deterministic core, generative periphery

**On screen**
- Crisis rule → Scoring → NLP → Retrieval → Generation
- Each layer degrades **independently**
- The layers that can fail unpredictably are exactly the ones the system can proceed without
- *(Figure 3.1 — system architecture)*

**Script (~90 s)**

> This is the pipeline, and the ordering is a safety property rather than an
> engineering convenience.
>
> First, a deterministic crisis rule. It runs before anything else — before the
> language model, and before anything is written to the database.
>
> Then psychometric scoring. Pure arithmetic, no external dependency; it always
> produces a result.
>
> Then the fine-tuned classifier, which adds a text-derived signal. If the model
> is unavailable, it falls back to lexicon matching.
>
> Then retrieval, then generation. These are last precisely because they are the
> parts that can fail unpredictably. If either fails, the system returns its
> deterministic results — it does not return an error, and it does not guess.
>
> So read the diagram from left to right as decreasing reliability and
> decreasing authority at the same time. The most trustworthy component runs
> first and can pre-empt everything after it.

*Ghi chú: đây là slide bạn nên nói chậm nhất trong Phần 1. "Thứ tự này là thuộc tính
an toàn, không phải tiện tay" — đó là câu đáng nhớ nhất của phần kiến trúc.*

## Slide 5 — Data and the ground-truth label

**On screen**
- 466 synthetic students · frozen split 326 / 70 / 70 · seed 42
- Label: 4-class, from **max severity** of DASS-21 stress subscale and PSS-10
- Test distribution — Low 16 · Moderate 23 · High 22 · Severe 9
- ⚠ The 4-class composite is **an invention of this project**, not a validated scale
- *(Figure 4.2 — label distribution)*

**Script (~80 s)**

> The evaluation data is synthetic — generated text plus generated questionnaire
> answers — and I will return to what that costs me on slide 13.
>
> The ground-truth label needs explaining because it is a design decision, not a
> given. Each student has a DASS-21 stress score and a PSS-10 score. Each maps
> to a severity band. I take the more severe of the two.
>
> That is deliberately conservative: in screening, a false alarm is cheaper than
> a miss. But I want to be clear that the resulting four-class scale is my own
> composite. DASS-21 is validated. PSS-10 is validated. The combination of the
> two into one four-class label carries no independent psychometric validation,
> and that is a limitation I state rather than one I have solved.
>
> The split is frozen and reused everywhere, because the classifier was
> fine-tuned on its training portion — redrawing it would leak training data
> into the test set.

## Slide 7 — The safety layer

**On screen**
- Deterministic rule, runs **before** the model and **before** persistence
- Rebuilt around six suicide-risk constructs, not a longer phrase list
- Held-out set: 60 bilingual items, **frozen before the rewrite**
- **Precision 0.600 → 1.000 · Recall 0.200 → 0.867 · F1 0.300 → 0.929**
- Indirect ideation **0/14 → 11/14** · zero false positives across 20 lethal-sounding idioms
- Crisis text is never stored — only the trigger reason

**Script (~110 s)**

> This is the slide I would most like you to remember, because it is the one
> where the project turned a weakness into a result.
>
> The crisis rule runs before the language model and before anything is written
> to the database — so a student who discloses self-harm gets helplines, and
> that disclosure is never persisted.
>
> The original rule matched a list of fixed phrases. On fifty hand-labelled
> Vietnamese items it scored precision 0.800, recall 0.500 — and on indirect
> ideation, one case out of nine. The sentence it missed: *"I just want to sleep
> for a very long time and never wake up again."* Nothing fired.
>
> Fixing that honestly was harder than fixing it. The twelve failures were
> already published, so adding them to the phrase list and re-measuring on the
> same fifty items would have been tuning against my own test set.
>
> So I did it in this order. First I wrote a **new** sixty-item bilingual set and
> froze it, with its hash recorded, **before** touching the rule. Then I rebuilt
> the rule around six constructs from suicide-risk assessment — explicit intent,
> passive death wish, existence negation, perceived burdensomeness, preparation,
> escape — writing the patterns from that taxonomy rather than from my failures.
> Then I measured once.
>
> On that held-out set: precision went from 0.600 to **1.000**, recall from 0.200
> to **0.867**. Indirect ideation went from zero out of fourteen to **eleven out
> of fourteen**. No false positives, across twenty idioms I wrote to be as
> lethal-sounding as I could make them — *"this deadline is literally killing
> me"*, *"khó muốn chết"*.
>
> Two things I want to say against my own result.
>
> The old rule was **worse than I had published**. On phrasing it had never seen
> it scored recall 0.200, not 0.500 — part of that original number came from the
> test set sharing wording with the lexicon.
>
> And I wrote both the patterns and the held-out set. The procedure was real, and
> the taxonomy is external, but a set written by an independent annotator would
> be stronger evidence. Three indirect items are still missed, and they are
> published verbatim.

*Ghi chú: đọc chậm câu tiếng Anh của ví dụ miss. Đây là khoảnh khắc mạnh nhất của bài —
bạn tự đưa ra bằng chứng chống lại chính mình, kèm số đo. Đừng xin lỗi, chỉ trình bày.*

## Slide 8 — Retrieval and grounding

**On screen**
- 23 chunks, curated knowledge base, multilingual embeddings
- Grounding is **absolute**: the model may rephrase retrieved material, never add to it
- Every suggestion carries a **chunk id**; the service **verifies** citations and drops invented ones
- Empty retrieval → the generator is **not called at all**

**Script (~85 s)**

> The advisory layer is retrieval-augmented, and the interesting part is what
> stops it inventing advice.
>
> Three mechanisms. First, the prompt rule is absolute — the model may rephrase
> the retrieved material to fit the student, but it may not add advice,
> techniques, services or phone numbers that are not in that material, even if
> it believes them to be true.
>
> Second, every suggestion must cite the chunk it came from, by id. The service
> then checks those citations against what was actually retrieved and **discards
> any the model invented**. So a fabricated source cannot reach the student.
>
> Third, and most importantly: if retrieval returns nothing, the generator is not
> called at all. The system says advice is unavailable rather than filling the
> gap from the model's own knowledge.
>
> That last one is the difference between a grounded system and a system that
> merely prefers to be grounded.

## Slide 10 — The measurement that changed the design

**On screen**
- 57 hand-labelled queries · **MRR 0.787** · Recall@1 0.658 · Recall@5 0.903
- Paired experiment on the 16 queries the query construction actually alters:

| Query construction | MRR | Recall@1 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Keywords only *(old)* | 0.509 | 0.344 |
| Student's sentence only | 0.778 | 0.656 |
| **Sentence + keywords** *(current)* | **0.839** | **0.719** |

**Script (~85 s)**

> This is my favourite result, because the measurement changed the system rather
> than just describing it.
>
> I built a labelled query set and measured retrieval: MRR 0.787, Recall@5 0.903.
>
> But the finding that mattered was about how the query was built. The original
> code threw away the student's own sentence and searched using the matched
> lexicon keywords instead, then appended a label like "student with High
> stress".
>
> Both decisions were wrong, and the measurement showed it. On the sixteen
> queries the construction actually changes: keywords alone scored 0.509 MRR. The
> student's sentence alone scored 0.778 — better than the engineered version.
> Sentence plus keywords, which is what the system does now, scores 0.839.
>
> Appending the stress label hurt every variant I tested. It is semantic noise
> that pulls the embedding away from the topic.
>
> The shape was chosen by running all six candidates through the harness, not by
> intuition — and the harness reproduces the before-and-after on every run, so a
> regression would be visible rather than silent.

**Script (~55 s)**

> Briefly, the performance picture, measured over thirty runs per stage.
>
> The entire deterministic pipeline — scoring, safety rule, lexicon, retrieval and
> the classifier — costs under 90 milliseconds at the median.
>
> The number I want to highlight is the crisis rule: **0.05 milliseconds**. Running

>

## Slide 13 — Limitations: the one that matters most

**On screen**
- Training and test text come from **41 templates**
- Exact-duplicate leakage: **zero** — the standard control passes
- Near-duplicate similarity: **median 0.799**, and **34 of 70** test items exceed 0.80
- Reproduce: `python scripts/check_leakage.py`
- ⇒ Every text-classification number here is a **pipeline demonstration**, not evidence of stress detection

**Script (~90 s)**

> If you take one limitation away from this talk, take this one.
>
> My text is generated from 41 Vietnamese sentence templates. The standard
> leakage control — checking for exact duplicates across splits — passes
> perfectly. Zero duplicates.
>
> That control is the wrong instrument here, and I can show you why with numbers.
> I measured each test item's maximum similarity to any training item. The median
> is 0.799. Thirty-four of the seventy test items exceed 0.80, and the measurement
> holds under three different similarity metrics.
>
> So the model is not being asked to detect stress. It is being asked to
> recognise templates it has already seen in a slightly different arrangement.
>
> The consequence is that every text-classification figure in this project is a
> demonstration that the pipeline works end to end — and is **not** evidence of
> real stress-detection ability. That is also the honest explanation for why
> TF-IDF wins on slide 9.
>
> This is why the real-data collection toolkit and an identical `--dataset real`
> evaluation path already exist in the codebase, waiting for participants.

*Ghi chú: nếu chỉ kịp nói 1 limitation thì nói cái này. Nó vừa là điểm yếu lớn nhất,
vừa là đóng góp #3 trong báo cáo (chứng minh exact-duplicate check là công cụ sai).*

## Slide 14 — What is not done

**On screen**
- Component ablation — **blocked on provider quota**, 38/70 already cached
- Faithfulness judging — does the answer actually use the passage it cites?
- Statistics — single seed, no confidence intervals, no significance test
- Instrument provenance — Vietnamese DASS-21/PSS-10 wording not traced to the validated versions
- Real participants — **zero**

**Script (~70 s)**

> To be complete, here is what I have not done.
>
> The component ablation is written, tested and wired to the same split — it is
> blocked on the free-tier daily token cap, not on code. 38 of 70 responses are
> already cached, so finishing it costs the remaining 32 requests.
>
> Faithfulness is unmeasured. I measure what was *retrieved*; I do not yet measure
> whether the sentence citing a passage is actually supported by it. That needs
> an LLM-as-judge pass.
>
> Every number comes from a single seed. No confidence intervals, no significance
> testing. So I cannot yet tell you whether the gap between two systems is real.
>
> The Vietnamese wording of both instruments is not traced item-by-item to the
> published validated translations. The arithmetic is provably correct; the
> linguistic validity is not established.
>
> And no real participants yet. That is the study, and it is the next step.

# Q&A preparation

*Ghi chú: phần này chỉ để bạn ôn, không lên slide. Mỗi câu trả lời nên bắt đầu bằng
sự thật, không phải bằng lời bào chữa.*

**Q: "Your title says LLM-powered. Which table evaluates the LLM?"**
> Table 4.4 has the zero-shot row, and section 4.6 evaluates retrieval over 57
> labelled queries. The full pipeline row is empty, and I explained why on slide
> 9 — a retrieval-path defect I found and fixed, then a provider quota limit. The
> ablation is written and cached; it is a quota problem, not a code problem.

**Q: "Why is TF-IDF beating your transformer?"**
> Because of my data, not my architecture. 41 templates, median near-duplicate
> similarity 0.799. N-grams excel at recognising recurring surface forms. I treat
> that result as evidence for the dataset critique on slide 13.

**Q: "Recall 0.50 on crisis detection. Would you deploy this?"**
> No — not as the only safety net, and not without changing the operating point.
> Right now it is one layer among several: the disclaimer is unavoidable,
> helplines appear regardless, and crisis text is never stored. But 0.50 recall is
> the wrong trade-off for this domain, and fixing it requires a new held-out set,
> because I have already measured against the one I have.

**Q: "The LLM could contradict the questionnaire. Which one does the student see?"**
> The questionnaire. Since the fusion rule was implemented, the headline level
> comes from the validated instrument whenever one exists; the model explains and
> suggests. Where the two differ, the student is told so explicitly.

**Q: "Your 4-class label is not a validated scale."**
> Correct, and I say so on slide 5. It is a conservative composite — the more
> severe of the DASS-21 and PSS-10 mappings. It has no independent psychometric
> validation, and validating it is outside this scope.

**Q: "How do I know your numbers are reproducible?"**
> Frozen split, seeded generator, disk-cached deterministic model responses, and
> the exact evaluated dataframe written to disk on every run. Two known gaps: the
> script that produced one intermediate CSV is missing from the repository, and
> dependency versions are unpinned. Both are in the report's appendix.

**Q: "What stops the model inventing a helpline number?"**
> Three things — slide 8. Absolute grounding in the prompt; citation ids verified
> by the service, which drops invented ones; and empty retrieval means the
> generator is never called. What I have not yet measured is faithfulness: whether
> the cited passage genuinely supports the sentence citing it.
